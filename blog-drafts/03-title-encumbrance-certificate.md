# Title Deed and Encumbrance Certificate: Understanding the Basics

**Slug:** title-deed-encumbrance-certificate-basics  
**Category:** Property Documentation  
**Excerpt:** Understand what title documents and an Encumbrance Certificate can tell you—and why both need careful review before buying land.

Property paperwork can feel complicated, especially when several documents refer to the same land. Two terms buyers commonly encounter are the title documents and the Encumbrance Certificate (EC). They serve different purposes, and neither should be treated as a complete guarantee on its own.

## What do title documents show?

Title documents help explain the ownership history of the property. A lawyer may review earlier sale deeds, gift or partition deeds, inheritance documents, court orders and other records relevant to how the present owner acquired the land. The review should confirm that the land description, survey numbers, boundaries and extent are consistent.

## What is an Encumbrance Certificate?

An EC is a search record of certain registered transactions or interests recorded against the property for the period and details searched. It can be useful for identifying recorded mortgages, sales or other entries. However, it may not show every possible claim, dispute, unregistered arrangement or defect in ownership.

## Why the search period matters

Ask for an EC covering a period advised by your lawyer based on the property's history. A short search period may not reveal older transactions that matter to the title chain. Check that the survey number, village, sub-registrar jurisdiction and property description used for the search are correct.

## Review the documents together

Do not review the EC in isolation. Compare its entries with the title deeds and the seller's claims. Check whether names, dates, document numbers, property extent and survey details match. Ask your lawyer to explain any mortgage, release, cancellation, mismatch or missing link in the chain.

## Questions to ask before proceeding

- Can the seller provide the title chain and supporting documents?
- Does the property description match across the documents?
- Does the EC cover the appropriate period and correct property details?
- Are there entries that require a release deed or further explanation?
- Have land records, possession and any relevant court matters been checked separately?
- Has an independent lawyer given a written opinion?

## Final thought

An EC is one useful part of due diligence, not a certificate that every legal risk has been eliminated. A complete review should consider title history, land records, approvals, possession and the terms of the proposed sale.
